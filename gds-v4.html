From the foundations of gravity to GDS v.4

GDS research began with an in-depth audit of the foundations of gravitational theory — from Newtonian gravity to Einstein’s General Relativity. We continue this line of inquiry by investigating nonlinear gravitational response, transitional states and the dynamic potential of the field which, according to our research hypothesis, is not fully captured in current cosmological descriptions.

From the outset, the guiding idea of GDS has been to explain observed dark-sector phenomena without requiring dark matter and dark energy as separate physical entities. In our model description, their role is taken by nonlinear gravitational-field dynamics. We advance the hypothesis that what is currently described as dark phenomena may reflect an incomplete description of gravitational response. We test this interpretation through calculations and comparison with observations; effective parameters in the numerical implementation do not in themselves imply a new form of matter.

GDS v.4 connects the current implementation of the GDS v.3 framework (Gravitational Dynamical System) with insights from GSC-X research into the stability of complex dynamical systems. It develops a shared description of gravitational response, transitions between regimes and the ability of a system to remain within a viable state over time.

The starting point is the original GDS v.3 implementation and its cosmological and galactic models. The new version advances field dynamics, the perturbation system and conservation laws while maintaining continuity with the verified limits of General Relativity.

Gravity as a dynamic response

The research centres on an effective gravitational interaction that varies with density, evolution and the spatial scale of a system. We therefore describe gravitational response through interconnected dynamical states and transitions rather than through a single fixed parameter alone. General Relativity already describes a dynamic spacetime; GDS investigates additional state, time and scale dependence of the effective gravitational coupling.

GDS investigates how different regimes emerge from the same dynamics: structure growth, long-term stability, reserve depletion, collapse and possible saturation. It interprets effects attributed to dark matter and dark energy as manifestations of the field without introducing them as separate physical entities.

The algorithmic kernel as a key to mapping dynamics

Our own dynamic algorithmic kernel, GSC-X, is a central research tool. It has enabled detailed numerical investigation of the complex systems studied, mapping their operating regimes and tracking emerging instability, transitions, reorganisation and recovery. We regard the precision and resolution with which these processes could be captured within the models and tested conditions as a key contribution.

This mapping was central to formulating our view of gravitational dynamics: according to our research hypothesis, its variability and transitional regimes represent an underexplored source of insights and possible explanations. The algorithmic kernel helps us identify and quantify these connections; conclusions transferred between systems are further assessed against their specific physics and the available data.

From stars to pulsars and magnetars

Research into stellar dynamics examines the interplay of gravity, pressure, plasma, energy transport and magnetic activity. Regenerative metastability offers a view of stellar longevity as the continuous organisation of instability and renewal of a viable operating corridor.

This developing direction includes different stellar types and compact objects — neutron stars, pulsars and magnetars. The aim is to distinguish stable and transitional regimes and investigate connections between internal dynamics, reorganisation and gravitational collapse. GSC-X insights guide state analysis; individual astrophysical mechanisms require their own physical modelling and evaluation.

Beyond the horizon: a finite black-hole core

Related GDS numerical studies model the continuation of collapse beyond the event horizon. They investigate the hypothesis that nonlinear saturation of gravitational response can limit further collapse and produce a finite dynamic core instead of a classical singularity.

These models allow transitional states, mass conservation and possible transfer into a causally disconnected expanding region to be examined. This is a mathematical and numerical investigation of black-hole interiors; its physical interpretation is further assessed through model consistency and testable consequences, including possible gravitational-wave ringdown signatures.

The early Universe and cosmological questions

The cosmological branch investigates whether a variable effective gravitational response can support faster structure formation in the early Universe and offer an explanation for the early appearance of massive galaxies. It connects this mechanism with later cosmic evolution and compares it with the standard ΛCDM description.

GDS v.3 provides the initial implementation for comparisons involving the cosmic microwave background, gravitational lensing and baryon acoustic oscillations. GDS v.4 develops these topics further; proposed explanations of cosmological discrepancies are assessed against specific data, statistical tests and predictions.

Current status of GDS v.4

GDS v.4 is a research implementation under development. It connects existing GDS v.3 numerical results, galactic-dynamics and finite-core studies with insights into regenerative metastability from GSC-X research. The next step is to integrate these branches into a consistent physical and mathematical description with reproducible outputs.

From understanding dynamics to technology

We regard numerical investigation of stellar models and black-hole interior models as important support for a research direction that places dynamics and transitions between states at its centre. It enables more detailed mapping of these systems, investigation of explanations for their behaviour and derivation of principles that can be further developed and tested in physical models and technologies involving complex dynamics.

This path from gravitational research to applied dynamical principles contributed to the development of the GSC-X executive algorithm. Across the completed cross-domain validation runs, we achieved 100% reproduction success across different engineering and scientific domains. This result applies to the tested configurations, metrics and conditions; together with the numerical studies, it supports further development of shared dynamical principles and their technological applications.
